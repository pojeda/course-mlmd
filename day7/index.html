Research Ethics and Responsible Use of AI in Chemistry¶

Artificial intelligence (AI) offers opportunities to support chemical research, from analysing experimental data and predicting molecular properties to reviewing scientific literature and suggesting experimental approaches. However, using AI in research also raises important ethical questions. Responsible scientific practice requires researchers to maintain accuracy, transparency, accountability, and research integrity. AI should therefore be regarded as a tool that supports scientific work, rather than a replacement for human expertise and critical judgement. As emphasised in Umeå University’s guidance on generative AI, researchers remain responsible for the reliability and scientific validity of their work, regardless of whether AI tools have been used.

Bias and reliability are important concerns when applying AI in chemistry. AI models learn from existing data, which may be incomplete, unrepresentative, or affected by biases in scientific publications. For example, an AI model trained predominantly on well-studied chemical compounds may produce less reliable predictions for novel molecules or underrepresented classes of materials. Generative AI tools can also produce convincing but incorrect explanations, calculations, or scientific references. Consequently, AI-generated suggestions concerning reaction mechanisms, molecular structures, or experimental procedures must be critically evaluated and, where appropriate, verified against established scientific literature, experimental evidence, and validated methods. AI-generated information should never be treated as scientific evidence without independent verification.

Data privacy and confidentiality are equally important. Chemical research may involve unpublished experimental results, confidential industrial collaborations, proprietary chemical information, or personal data. Uploading such material to an external AI service may expose information to unauthorised parties or conflict with data protection regulations, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Umeå University therefore requires users to assess the sensitivity of information before entering it into AI systems and to ensure that the chosen service is approved for the relevant type of data. Researchers must also consider copyright restrictions when uploading scientific articles, unpublished manuscripts, or other protected material. When uncertain, they should seek guidance before sharing information with an AI tool.

Research integrity and transparency require researchers to be open about how AI contributes to their scientific work. AI may assist with improving scientific writing, developing computer code, exploring hypotheses, or creating illustrations, but researchers must critically review the results and remain responsible for their accuracy. Presenting AI-generated analyses, fabricated references, or invented experimental findings as genuine research undermines scientific integrity. Umeå University’s Faculty of Science and Technology policy for doctoral education specifically requires transparent disclosure of generative AI use, including an AI declaration in doctoral theses. More generally, documenting which tools were used, for what purposes, and how their outputs were verified supports reproducibility and scientific accountability. Researchers should also follow applicable course instructions, journal policies, and publication requirements.

Reflecting on responsible AI use is an essential part of developing good scientific judgement. Consider, for example, a researcher who uses generative AI to suggest a synthetic pathway, interpret experimental results, or draft part of a chemistry report. What ethical risks might arise from this use? Could the AI-generated information contain errors or biases? Is the researcher permitted to share the underlying experimental data with the AI service? How should the AI contribution be verified and acknowledged? These questions illustrate why responsible AI use involves more than obtaining useful results: it also requires protecting research data, evaluating scientific reliability, maintaining transparency, and taking personal responsibility for the conclusions presented.
